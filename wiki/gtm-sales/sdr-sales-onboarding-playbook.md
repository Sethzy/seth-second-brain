# SDR Sales Onboarding Playbook

## Overview

The core lesson across the 30MPC material is that strong outbound is a connected operating system. Account selection determines whether research is worth doing. Research supplies context for email, LinkedIn, voicemail, and calls. Calls create interest in a problem rather than explain a product. Discovery moves from the buyer's current situation to operational pain, executive consequences, and business impact. Multithreading turns one person's interest into organizational consensus. Managers make the motion repeatable through focused practice, deal inspection, and metrics.

The system rewards disciplined relevance over raw activity. Volume still matters, but only inside a narrow ICP, with a small set of real problems, consistent messaging across channels, protected prospecting time, and fast inspection of what converts.

## The Onboarding Outcome

By day 30, an SDR should be able to:

- identify the narrow account and persona “slot” where meetings can become real revenue;
- research one useful trigger and reuse it coherently across a multichannel sequence;
- write a short, problem-led email with relevant proof and a low-friction CTA;
- open a cold call with context, deliver a problem proposition, absorb an objection, and sell the next conversation;
- protect two daily prospecting blocks and prepare tomorrow's list before the day ends;
- hand off meetings with a clear problem hypothesis, stakeholder context, and reason to act;
- distinguish a pleasant conversation from a problem worth solving now;
- use a shared language for activity, conversion, call quality, and coaching.

## Module 1: Choose The Slot Before Increasing Activity

The “slot” is the narrow combination of company profile, buyer seniority, problem, and deal shape where opportunities tend to move both large and fast. The 60-day overhaul case improved performance by closing weak opportunities, banning meetings outside the slot, and accepting fewer total meetings in exchange for materially higher conversion.

For an SDR, the practical rule is:

1. Define the ICP band: company size, industry, maturity, geography, stack, and relevant trigger.
2. Define the minimum persona: the person close enough to the problem and senior enough to mobilize action.
3. Define the two largest problems the product solves for that persona.
4. Define disqualifiers and anti-ICP signals.
5. Track conversion by slot, not only aggregate activity.

Pipeline should be real enough to learn from. Junk meetings create false reassurance, waste downstream capacity, and hide whether the message works.

## Module 2: Build One Problem-Led Message System

The outbound sequence should reuse one strong piece of research instead of collecting a different personalization fact for every touch. One company trigger can power the first email, LinkedIn context, voicemail, and tailored call opener.

### The Four-Sentence Email

The default email has four parts:

1. Personalization or trigger: what changed or what was observed.
2. Problem: the specific operational problem the trigger is likely to create.
3. Solution and proof: one sentence on how the problem is solved, ideally with relevant social proof.
4. Interest CTA: make replying easier than evaluating a calendar request.

The message should fit on a phone screen, avoid feature lists, and use a subject line that looks like an internal business topic. “No selling in the subject line” means the subject is about the buyer's world, without deception.

Executive outreach sharpens this further:

- remove “we,” “our,” and “us” language that makes the email about the vendor;
- connect the trigger to risk or cost of inaction;
- use unsure tonality when presenting a specific hypothesis, inviting correction rather than pretending certainty;
- offer an idea exchange or relevant example rather than immediately asking for time.

### Sequence Design

A useful default is 10-14 touches over roughly 30 days across email, phone, voicemail, and LinkedIn. Start with the largest problem. Give the researched message two low-friction bubble-ups. If it does not resonate, switch problems instead of repeating the same pitch.

The channel jobs differ:

- Email carries the clearest problem proposition.
- LinkedIn starts with a blank connection request, then points back to the context without pasting a full pitch.
- A cold call tests whether the problem is live and earns a conversation.
- A voicemail directs attention back to the email; it does not primarily ask for a callback.

The recommended pressure pattern is “nudge, nudge, push away, push away.” Later messages lower pressure, check whether the prospect is the right person, or close the loop. The sequence should feel coordinated, not like four independent automations.

## Module 3: Run The Cold Call

### Context Before Permission

Generic permission openers decay as prospects learn to recognize them. The durable element is context: the first words should show why this person was called.

Two usable patterns are:

- Social-proof opener: mention relevant peers or nearby customers, introduce yourself, and ask whether your name has been tossed around.
- Tailored permission opener: lead with the researched trigger, acknowledge the cold call, and ask for a short window to explain the specific reason for calling.

Exact wording matters less than confident, human delivery. A rep who sounds calm, specific, and willing to be rejected will outperform one reciting the technically “perfect” script without conviction.

### The Problem Proposition

The pitch should spend most of its time on a triggering problem and very little on the product:

1. Describe the problem in the prospect's language.
2. Show the operational or emotional consequence.
3. Give one sentence on how the company solves it, with enough proof to be credible.
4. Ask whether the prospect is open to learning more at a better time.

The cold call is not the place to sell the car. It sells the test drive: enough relevance and curiosity to justify the next conversation.

### The Mr. Miyagi Objection Method

Pressure creates resistance. When the buyer objects, first absorb the momentum instead of arguing:

1. Agree, validate, or redirect without defensiveness.
2. Incentivize conversation with a diagnostic question.
3. Use the answer to sell the specific next step, not the entire product.

For a vague “not interested,” the rep can lower pressure and ask which explanation is closest: something is already in place, the problem is not important, or the prospect simply dislikes cold calls. The objective is a truthful diagnosis, not winning an argument.

### Cold-Call Scorecard

Coach the controllables on every call:

- context appeared before the ask;
- tone was confident and conversational;
- the problem occupied more airtime than the solution;
- the rep stayed calm after the first objection;
- at least one useful diagnostic question was asked;
- the meeting was positioned as a test drive;
- the call produced either a next step or better account intelligence.

## Module 4: Protect The Prospecting Machine

The productivity system is built around energy and batching:

- Green hours, roughly morning to noon: complete the hardest prospecting work, especially cold calls.
- Yellow hours, roughly noon to 3 p.m.: customer and prospect meetings.
- Red hours, roughly 3 to 6 p.m.: research, list building, CRM, and administration.

For an SDR, the second prospecting block can replace much of the yellow block. The non-negotiable is that a morning call/email block may move within green hours but should not be deleted.

Three principles make the calendar work:

1. Front-load the hardest activity before interruptions accumulate.
2. Use Parkinson's law deliberately: assign a realistic time box so research and admin do not expand indefinitely.
3. Stack similar tasks to preserve momentum and reduce context switching.

Tomorrow's call list is built in today's red hours. Building the list after opening the inbox burns the highest-energy prospecting window.

## Module 5: Hand Off A Meeting That Can Convert

An SDR handoff should give the AE a hypothesis, not merely a booking notification:

- account and persona fit against the slot;
- trigger and original problem proposition;
- what the prospect confirmed, rejected, or corrected;
- relevant current process or tooling;
- operational consequence and any early business impact;
- known stakeholders and missing stakeholders;
- stated timing, initiative, or reason to act;
- objections already surfaced;
- exact next-step commitment.

This prevents the AE from restarting discovery and signals whether the meeting is likely to become revenue.

## Module 6: Understand Discovery Well Enough To Source Better Meetings

Even if the SDR does not own full discovery, the discovery tree improves qualification and messaging. The four levels are:

1. Situation: how the work happens today.
2. Operational problem: what is painful, slow, risky, or expensive for the champion.
3. Executive problem: the consequence a functional leader is accountable for.
4. Business impact: a C-level metric, existential risk, or major initiative threatened by the problem.

The first full discovery call uses a 5-20-5 structure:

- First five minutes: trigger-based business rapport and a purpose-plan-outcome agenda.
- Middle 20 minutes: move vertically through one or more discovery trees while sharing relevant points of view, examples, praise, playbacks, and “pile-ons.”
- Final five minutes: validate whether the problem is worth solving, whether it is worth solving now, who else is involved, and what the next step should be.

The key anti-pattern is horizontal interrogation: asking many unrelated situational questions without going deeper. The seller should start as close to a problem as possible, ask what moment made it visible, use humble disclaimers before sensitive questions, and connect the operational issue to an executive consequence.

## Module 7: Multithread From Interest To Consensus

One enthusiastic contact is not a deal. A champion is valuable when they can expose friction, map the buying group, and help mobilize others.

The “three-headed Hydra” offers a simple target: build support across three complementary stakeholders before relying on executive approval. The exact roles vary, but the pattern often combines a functional leader, an adjacent department or enablement owner, and another segment leader.

The multithreading sequence is:

1. Align with the champion on a one-page problem statement: objective, threatening problem, current approach, and negative consequences.
2. Ghostwrite the internal meeting invitation so the champion has low-friction language to mobilize peers.
3. Ask who is skeptical, who protects the status quo, what political dynamics matter, and which competing projects could absorb budget or attention.
4. Prepare the champion to participate visibly instead of leaving the seller alone against the group.
5. Open the group meeting around the shared problem, not a product pitch.
6. Recap existing discovery, roll the new stakeholder's perspective into the problem, demo only what is relevant, and align the group on whether to act.

A “champion” who insists nobody is skeptical and no competing priority exists is giving a red flag, not reassurance.

## Module 8: Demo Only What Discovery Earned

The “Pimp My Ride” demo is a forcing function: every feature shown must connect to something the buyer said in discovery. The seller should be able to say, in effect, “you told me this; therefore I am showing you that.”

This keeps discovery and demo connected, prevents feature dumping, and makes the buyer feel understood. The demo should show how the product changes the specific operational problem and supports the stated business outcome. Broader capability can be introduced later when it expands a validated use case.

## Module 9: Learn Commercial Discipline Early

The negotiation material uses four levers behind pricing:

- how much the customer buys;
- how fast the customer pays;
- how long the customer commits;
- how predictable the deal timing is.

Every concession request follows three steps:

1. Be human: ask why the buyer needs the concession.
2. Review all four levers.
3. Walk through options that trade value rather than donate it.

Discounts should pay the customer for something valuable to the seller: more volume, faster cash, longer commitment, or agreed timing. A request for monthly billing, a shorter term, half the initial volume, termination for convenience, or a held price changes one or more levers and therefore changes the commercial structure.

The SDR does not negotiate contracts, but should learn the doctrine: never invent pricing, never promise a concession, record the buyer's underlying reason, and bring commercial pressure into the process early enough that it does not become a surprise at the finish line.

## Manager Operating System For The First 30 Days

### Operating System

Define the slot, approved problems, sequence, call framework, handoff fields, discovery language, qualification rules, and CRM standards. Reps should not have to infer what “good” looks like.

### Operating Rhythm

- Daily: two protected prospecting blocks and a short end-of-day list-prep/learning review.
- Twice weekly: call role-play on one micro-skill, not an entire methodology.
- Weekly: scorecard review covering activity, connects, conversations, meetings, show rate, accepted opportunities, and qualitative call quality.
- Weekly: pipeline/account review that verifies CRM data, assesses risk, and assigns a concrete next step.
- Biweekly: one-on-one reserved primarily for relationship, motivation, career development, and rep-specific support—not a disguised deal review.

Training should stay on one skill long enough to stick. Rotating from discovery to forecasting to prospecting every week creates exposure without competence.

### Metrics

Use a conversion ladder rather than activity alone:

1. Accounts activated.
2. Touches completed by channel.
3. Connect rate.
4. Conversation rate.
5. Set rate.
6. Show rate.
7. AE-accepted or qualified opportunity rate.
8. Pipeline and revenue sourced.

The book's public description and cold-call course highlight the “Golden 3” of connect, set, and show rates. The full operating system adds downstream acceptance and revenue so the team does not optimize for meetings that never convert.

## Recommended Pre-Boarding And 30-Day Plan

### Before Joining

1. Watch the productivity, outbound-sequence, and cold-call modules.
2. Write a one-page summary of the slot, two buyer problems, and the message architecture.
3. Draft one email, one tailored permission opener, one problem proposition, one voicemail, and one objection response.
4. Build a sample calendar with protected green and red hours.

### Week 1: Context And Certification

- Learn product, customers, proof, ICP, anti-ICP, personas, CRM, and approved claims.
- Listen to strong and weak call examples.
- Pass written certification on the two core problems and the handoff schema.
- Role-play opener, problem proposition, and first objection separately.

### Week 2: Controlled Production

- Work a small, manager-approved account set.
- Launch the first coordinated multichannel sequence.
- Review calls daily and rewrite only the weak component.
- Measure connect, conversation, set, and show rates from the first live cohort.

### Week 3: Scale With Quality

- Increase account and dial volume only if message quality and CRM hygiene hold.
- Introduce the second problem and push-away messages.
- Practice discovery-tree qualification and stronger meeting handoffs.
- Begin tracking AE acceptance and sourced opportunity quality.

### Week 4: Independent Operation

- Run full green/yellow/red calendar discipline without manager reminders.
- Present a conversion review: what is working by persona, trigger, problem, channel, and objection.
- Demonstrate one full call from opener through objection and next step.
- Propose one evidence-backed improvement to the sequence or enablement system.

## The Essence

1. Relevance starts with account selection. More activity outside the slot creates noise, not pipeline.
2. Lead with the buyer's problem. Product language makes sense only after the problem has context.
3. Research once, reuse coherently. One strong trigger should unify every channel.
4. Earn the next step. A cold call sells a better conversation, not a complete solution.
5. Reduce pressure to get truth. Agreement, push-aways, unsure tonality, and silence reveal more than aggressive persuasion.
6. Protect prospecting time. The hardest revenue activity happens before inboxes and admin take over.
7. Qualify vertically. Move from situation to operational pain, executive consequence, and business impact.
8. One contact is fragile. Real deals require a champion, a buying group, and explicit consensus around the problem.
9. Trade; do not donate. Every concession should exchange value across volume, cash, commitment, or timing.
10. Managers own repeatability. Scripts matter, but the operating rhythm, scorecards, call review, and deliberate practice make them real.
