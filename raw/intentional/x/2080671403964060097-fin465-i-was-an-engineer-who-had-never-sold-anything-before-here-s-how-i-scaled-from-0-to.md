i was an engineer who had never sold anything before.

here's how I scaled from 0 to $1m arr in our first few months, closing f500s without any sales experience.

everyone says “sales/distribution is the moat” but I actually think its the opposite

the tl;dr: if your product is good enough, you don't need a sales team to sell enterprise. you need a good system to let the product do the talking.

here are the 8 principles we followed.

1/ product-first sales means the demo IS the close

every f500 call started the same way - i'd share my screen and show them origami working on their own data, live, in the first 5 minutes. 

no slidesv/vpitch deck. just the product solving their exact problem in front of them.

if your product can't sell itself in a 30-minute screen share, a sales team won't fix that.

2/ find the 3 people inside every company who get it

enterprise doesn't buy as a monolith. inside every f500 there are 3 people who instinctively understand why your product matters. 

your job is to find them, not convince the other 4,000 employees.

we found ours through outbound (using @origamichat), referrals from early users, and one accidental cold dm that turned into our biggest customer.

3/ pricing is a product decision, not a sales decision

we launched with a single price on the website. enterprise prospects saw the same page as everyone else. 

this isnt what most in B2B do, but it meant every call started with "we already know what it costs, here's what we need" instead of "how much does it normally go for?" which can take half the call negotiating

4/ the most dangerous person in your pipeline is a polite maybe

we killed any deal that didn't have a champion with decision-making authority by the second meeting. 

polite interest from someone who wants to "loop in their team" is a time black hole.

f500s move fast when they want to. if they're not moving, they don't want it enough.

5/ ship the feature the f500 asks for, but hide it behind a plan gate

enterprise prospects will tell you exactly what they need to buy. built features for them specifically - but put those features on a plan tier, not a custom contract.

now every enterprise prospect sees a roadmapped product that already has what they need, and they self-select into the right plan. no custom dev/ bespoke pricing.

6/ your customer support team is your best sales team

our support team hears every objection before sales does. when prospects said "we already use hubspot" and support showed them how origami integrates, that was a sale that never touched a sales call.

we started routing many key trial users to a human in their first hour. not a salesperson but a support person who helps them get value. 

that single hour is worth more than 10 follow-up emails imo.

7/ don't hire a salesperson until you personally can't keep up

i did every enterprise demo myself for the first 3 months. this is the part founders hate hearing because it feels unscalable, but it's the only way to learn what actually closes.

the pattern recognition from doing 500 demos yourself is irreplaceable. you can't delegate what you don't understand.

8/ enterprise will pay for speed, not features

every f500 we closed told us the same thing: "we could build this ourselves in 6 months. we'd rather pay you and have it working next week."

speed is your unfair advantage as a startup. don't compete on feature count. compete on how fast you can solve their problem right now.

---------------
that's it. 8 principles and no sales team. build a product that works and follow this process

if you're a technical founder scared of enterprise sales, start with a demo on their own data. it's the closest thing to a cheat code i've found.

if this is useful, save it + ill post more tactics like this daily
