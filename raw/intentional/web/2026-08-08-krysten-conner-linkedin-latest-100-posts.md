Strategies for: 
--> Deciding which deals to focus on

--> Deciding which deals to focus on 
 
Companies have ICPs: Ideal Customer Profiles 
 
I have IOPs: Ideal Opportunity Profiles 
 
When I have to decide how much time, research, and personalization to devote, I give each deal a "grade". 
 
I've narrowed down 8 characteristics of deals that are most likely to close. 
 
I assign points to each characteristic. 
 
My point system looks like this: 
 
 
--> There's a Champion Who Gives the "Inside Scoop": 10 points 
--> They have Specific Goals with Metrics for Each: 10 points 
-->They Invest Heavily in Tech Related to My Product: 10 points 
--> They are Responsive to Calls, Texts, Email: 10 points 
--> There are a Low Number of Obstacles: 10 points 
--> They have Lots of Potential End Users: 15 points 
--> They have a Compelling Event: 20 points 
--> They make Decisions Quickly: 15 points 
 
 
The higher the grade, the more time and energy I spend on it. 
 
 
 
--> When to give up & move on 
 
I use a "3 strikes You're Out" rule. 
 
If there is no response after I reach out 3 times 
 
across 3 different channels (email, text, LinkedIn, phone, Slack, etc.) 
 
I close it out.

But I've spent 9 years watching Enterprise deals die in Finance. And I've learned what CFOs actually need to hear.

Here are the 8 questions every CFO is asking behind closed doors, courtesy of the "Only CFOs" newsletter, as highlighted by Nate Nasralla :

1. Why do we need [X product] now? We were fine before. 
2. What bucket does this fall into, and can you quantify it?
-----Improves efficiencies
-----Increases revenue
-----Mission critical (e.g. cybersecurity)
-----"Candy": nice-to-have tool that doesn't really fit into the above groups 
3. Is the spend in the budget? If not, how will you make up the difference? 
4. How does this tool tie into our company objectives for this year? 
5. What tools do we already have that do something similar? And if similar tools exist, why can't we use those? 
6. Implementation: What are the people / time requirements? 
7. Support costs: Will we need to upgrade soon to enterprise /other tier? 
8. Pricing:
-----If usage-based pricing: I want a detailed analysis on how pricing works and modeling for what our planned usage will look like.
-----If seat-based pricing: I don't want to buy a bunch of extra seats for planned growth. That got a lot of people in trouble when headcount growth halted and layoffs happened.

The deal-killers? Numbers 1, 3, and 5.

2 of the 3 tech unicorns I've worked at train their AEs on 2 core concepts that salespeople don't typically learn: 
 
 
--> Towards v. Away language 
--> Above the Line (ATL) v. Below the Line (BTL) 
 
 
1) Towards v. Away language 
 
-> 80% of people are motivated by moving Away from Pain (more people would run from a German Shepherd than towards a Gold medal*) 
 
-> But sales reps talk about Gain, aka moving Towards: "time back in your day", efficiency & ROI 
 
 
Here's how it sounds IRL at UserGems 💎 : 
 
-> Towards sounds like, "Because my product automatically detects buying signals like hiring, promotions, and champion job changes...your sales reps will save 29% of their time and you'll see a 18x ROI." 
 
-> Away sounds like, "What goes away [by using my product] is sales rep's spending hours on manual research, low morale from not seeing results, and the frustration of your team missing their number." 
 
 
->>>TL;DR = talk about what problems go away 
 
 
 
 
2) Above the Line (ATL) v. Below the Line (BTL) 
 
-> "The Line" is the 'power line', so Directors, VPs, CXOs = Above the Line 
 
-> BTL thinks about their team v. ATL thinks about market share & risk 
 
 
IRL / UserGems example: 
 

-> ATL value prop sounds like, "With [my product] you reduce 3 risks: Your top competitor doesn't get in the door first, you don't lose more market share, and you don't have to explain your inaction to investors." 

-> BTL value prop sounds like, "What goes away [with my product] is worrying about too little pipeline, missing quota & losing your job." 
 
 
 
->>> TL;DR = choose the right language for your audience 
 
 
 
It takes time and effort to wrap our heads around these. 
 
But there's a reason the unicorns teach them. They win deals.

2 of the 3 tech unicorns I've worked at train their AEs on 2 core concepts that salespeople don't typically learn: 
 
 
--> Towards v. Away language 
--> Above the Line (ATL) v. Below the Line (BTL) 
 
 
1) Towards v. Away language 
 
-> 80% of people are motivated by moving Away from Pain (more people would run from a German Shepherd than towards a Gold medal*) 
 
-> But sales reps talk about Gain, aka moving Towards: "time back in your day" & ROI 
 
 
Here's how it sounds IRL at UserGems 💎 : 
 
-> Towards sounds like, "Because my product automatically detects buying signals like champion job changes & promotions, your sales reps will save 29% of their time and you'll see a 18x ROI." 
 
-> Away sounds like, "What goes away [by using my product] is sales rep's spending hours on manual research, the low morale from not seeing results, and the frustration of your team missing their number." 
 
 
->>>TL;DR = talk about what problems go away 
 
 
 
 
2) Above the Line (ATL) v. Below the Line (BTL) 
 
-> "The Line" is the 'power line', so Directors, VPs, CXOs = Above the Line 
 
-> BTL thinks about their team v. ATL thinks about market share & risk 
 
 
IRL / UserGems example: 
 
-> BTL value prop sounds like, "What goes away [with my product] is worrying about too little pipeline, missing quota & sales reps quitting." 
 
-> ATL value prop sounds like, "With [my product] you reduce 3 risks: Your top competitor doesn't get in the door first, you don't lose more market share, and you don't have to explain your inaction to investors." 
 
 
->>> TL;DR = choose the right language for your audience 
 
 
 
It takes time and effort to wrap our heads around these. 
 
But there's a reason the unicorns teach them. They win deals.
