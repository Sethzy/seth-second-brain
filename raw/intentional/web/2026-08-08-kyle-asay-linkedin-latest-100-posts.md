I refuse to forecast a deal if any of these 3 things are true:

1) No velocity

When a prospect delays next steps for weeks (or longer) they aren’t spending that time justifying your product - they are spending that time working on more important issues.

And if your product doesn’t address their most important problems, it’s not getting purchased.


2) No concerns

Buying carries risk. If your prospect isn’t asking you to help address concerns, they aren’t really considering buying.

No concerns often means no consideration.


3) No “next best option”

Execs rarely put all eggs in one basket (especially with the price of eggs today!) No alternative options to your product most likely means they haven’t committed to solve the problem you address.

If you are like most AEs, 50%+ of your deals have one (or more) of these “signs of trouble.”

Discovery calls end with “can you send me info and I’ll get back to you?” unless you break from the “normal” structure. The typical (broken) discovery goes like this:

- First five minutes: Intros and small talk

- Next ten minutes: Superficial discussion of the buyer’s day-to-day

- Next ten minutes: Seller shares high-level overview of product.

Twenty-five minutes into a thirty minute call and almost no progress is made. The seller barely knows anything about the buyer. The buyer barely knows anything about the seller’s product.

The final few minutes become a mad scramble for the seller to find something valuable enough to earn another call, but most discoveries end with the buyer saying, “Can you send me a case study and more information so we can review and get back to you?”

A great discovery looks like this:

- First five minutes: Intros and objective set

- Next five minutes: Point-of-view validated (or corrected) and priorities confirms

- Next ten minutes: Deep discovery of how the buyer is approaching their priorities

- Last ten minutes: Seller shares how they can help accomplish priorities and next steps for evaluation finalized

Maybe all you need is the “outline” and you can go run better discoveries.

But maybe you need a guide that breaks it down step-by-step.

If you want the step-by-step guide to great discovery, my discovery playbook is free today: https://lnkd.in/gT7ZMQQz

1) “What accomplishment are you most proud of?”

Incredible people have done incredible things and will continue doing incredible work.

Every AE can define a champion.

Great AEs are audible ready with champions they’ve built (vs inherited) with detailed stories of how it happened.

Ten SaaS sales realities I’ve learned the hard way:

1) If you only hear good news, you are losing the deal. All deals have risk. Champions share risk. No risk shared means no champion. No champion means you lose.

2) The longer it takes to close a deal, the longer your buyer has to realize they are ok without your solution. No velocity, no deal. Time really does kill deals. 

3) Solving a problem doesn’t win deals. Solving THE problem does. Every exec has hundreds of fires burning - they only pay money and spend time to stop the fires that threaten to burn the whole thing down. 

4) More people constantly getting added to the evaluation committee isn’t often a good thing - it means nobody wants to take ownership of the decision.

5) Your buyers spend most of their calls with you multi-tasking. If you don’t quickly capture their attention and keep them constantly engaged, your calls are a waste of time.

6) No one believes your ROI projections. If the business case isn’t in your buyer’s language with their numbers, it’s not taken seriously.

7) Buyers are biased against sellers. Any time you sound the same as other sellers, you strengthen that bias. If you want to win, learn to sound different.

8) You can’t create compelling events (end-of-quarter discounts don’t count). The only consistent way to accelerate deals is to make the buyer’s status quo unbearable.

9) A poorly run deal with a strong executive connection beats a well-run deal with no executive connection. If you have a six or seven-figure deal, and you’re not talking to the C-Level, you’re going to get blindsided by the competitor that is.

10) There is nothing more important to cover at the end of the call than selling next steps. Much better to skip agenda items and align on next steps than to finish the agenda and take next steps “offline.”

One of the least “fair” sales realities is that average sellers in great environments usually earn more than great sellers in average environments. Here are 3 ways to identify a great environment (starting with 2 critical metrics):

1) Gross Retention Rate

Gross retention rate is how well you preserve your existing renewal base. Basic example: If a company has 10 customers spending $10,000 on average, they have a renewal baseline of $100,000. 

If one customer that spends $10,000 doesn’t renew, their gross retention is $90,000, or 90%.

90%+ is good. 95%+ is great. 

2) Net Retention Rate

Net retention measures how much your customer revenue base grows each year. After you take out the lost renewals, and add in the renewals + expansion, is your customer base growing or shrinking?

110% is good. 115%+ is great. 120%+ is best-in-class. 

3) Quality of Sales Org

There are a lot of tools to identify the quality of a sales org (Glassdoor, RepVue), etc.

These are great places to start your research, but I know of sales orgs with great reviews where the team is miserable, and sales orgs where scores are “meh” but people are happy and crushing their comp plans.

So validate what you are seeing with the people that know best: current AEs at the company you are evaluating. Try and talk to at least three, otherwise you may talk to an outlier.
