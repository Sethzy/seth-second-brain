My Top 10 Highlights from The CEO Blog Every Enterprise Seller Should Be Reading ​ Dave Kellogg writes for CEOs and boards, not sellers, which is exactly the point of this doc: capturing how the people you sell to actually think and operate. Here’s my top 10. ​ Part 1: How to talk to executives ​ 1. Answer the question, then stop.​ ●​ Execs are direct, they work fast. And they typically show up with a line of questioning in their mind to run through. So when you hear, "How's the team doing, scale of 1 to 10?" the right answer isn't a "Oh, well you know..." type paragraph. It's, "7, but there are one or two key problems to work out." It answers the question, but leaves an open thread for them to pull on, if they care. ●​ Problem is, most sellers do the opposite. When a CFO asks, "What does implementation take?" they'll get a 90-second methodology tour, while the CFO just hears static, and wanted specifics. ●​ Do this instead: answer in < 10 seconds, leaving an open thread. "Six weeks, one dependency on your data team could stretch it." If they care, they'll pull the thread. If they don't, you just protected their line of questioning and attention. That's how you'll earn the next meeting and airtime. ●​ More here: https://www.kellblog.com/the-one-key-to-dealing-with-senior-executives-answer​ 2. Be the “simplifier” in the room.​ ●​ What does a complexifier call a simplifier? "Boss." Kellogg’s got a test for spotting complexifiers, too: slow progress, blamed on everything being difficult, propped up by prerequisites that sound “plausible” but on closer look, just aren't. ●​ Problem is, sellers run their own version of this all the time. Overbuilt mutual action plans, jargon-loaded value decks, "it depends" answers on pricing. And execs read complexity as one of two things: incompetence, or evasion. https://www.kellblog.com/the-one-key-to-dealing-with-senior-executives-answer-the-question/

5. You can't fix a compound metric.​ ●​ Kellogg's line: you can't fix a CAC payback period. It's a compound metric, and at least five different things could be driving it. Investors love compound metrics (CAC payback, Rule of 40, NRR). Operators can only act on atomic ones. ●​ Here's why that matters to you: accountability gets set on atomic metrics. OKRs get set on atomic metrics. Which means budget gets spent on atomic metrics. ●​ Do this instead: pitch the atomic metric someone in the room personally owns. Win rate, cycle time, ACV, rep ramp. Then show how it rolls up to the compound number the CFO reports. Two layers, that are explicitly connected: "We move win rate. Win rate is one of five inputs to your CAC payback problem." ●​ More here: https://www.kellblog.com/you-cant-fix-a-cac-payback-period-the-operator-vs​ 6. Learn the leaky bucket, and the topline formula.​ ●​ Good board decks open with the same equation every time: ​ ​ Starting ARR + new ARR - churned ARR = ending ARR. Shown across trailing quarters, so every number carries its own context. His point: you’ll never meet a CFO afraid of a table of numbers. What execs fear is numbers without comparison points. A stat with no plan %, no YoY, no trend is just noise. ●​ The “bucket” idea is also the mental model. Everything happening inside their business is either pouring water in (new ARR) or plugging leaks (churn). Every initiative, every vendor, every hire. ●​ Do this instead: read the prospect's business through the bucket idea before the first exec call. Is their problem new ARR or churn? And when you present impact numbers, give them context the same way: vs plan, vs last year, vs trend. ●​ More here: https://www.kellblog.com/the-first-three-slides-of-a-saas-board-deck-with-comp any-key-metrics/ https://www.kellblog.com/you-cant-fix-a-cac-payback-period-the-operator-vs-investor-view-of-saas-metrics/ https://www.kellblog.com/the-first-three-slides-of-a-saas-board-deck-with-company-key-metrics/ https://www.kellblog.com/the-first-three-slides-of-a-saas-board-deck-with-company-key-metrics/

​ How to structure the narrative 7. Start with what's needed, not what you have.​ ●​ Kellogg's favorite casualty: the rep who kept referencing Coca-Cola to Delta Airlines until the customer finally snapped. "Stop telling us about Coke. We are Delta. We fly airplanes." ●​ Problem is, recycled decks broadcast one message before you've said a word: this meeting is about your convenience, not their situation. Execs smell it in the first 90 seconds. ●​ Do this instead: for any meeting that matters, open a blank file. Not "open recent." Write the outline from the audience backward: who's in the room, and what do they need to walk out believing. Only then go hunting for existing slides that serve that outline. Structure first, slides last. ●​ More here: https://www.kellblog.com/stop-making-the-1-mistake-in-presentations/​ 8. Structure the message as diagnosis -> beliefs -> actions.​ ●​ “Given diagnosis X, and beliefs Y, we choose policy Z, and actions 1 through 5.” ​ ​ The idea is that facts get agreement fast. Then the interesting debate is never about the facts, it's about the beliefs. What happens next if nothing changes? That's also where the buying decision lives. ●​ Do this: structure your champion's internal narrative in a similar way. Diagnosis: what's true today, in their numbers. Belief: what we think happens next if nothing changes. Policy: the approach. Actions: the plan. Then borrow his future look-back in discovery: "It's three years out and this worked. Tell me why?" ●​ More here: https://www.kellblog.com/strategy-as-a-series-of-beliefs/ ​ 9. Speak in probabilities, not vibes.​ ●​ A deal slips, and the VP "adds risk" to the forecast without changing the number. Kellogg's response: adding risk is meaningless. You took a 90% number and turned it into a 60% number. ●​ A good system is probabilistic. At the deal level: commit = 90%, forecast = 70%, upside = 30%. Miss your forecast once every 10 quarters and you're calibrated. Miss it 2 of 4 and you're handing over a 50% number and calling it 90%. ●​ Do this instead: bring the same rigor to deal reviews. "This closes March 31 at 70%. Here's the one event that moves it to 90." Then track your own hit rate by category over time. A rep who actually closes 80% of forecast deals across 8 quarters has something almost nobody has: a credible forecast.
