John McMahon, the author of The Qualified Sales Leader, has an analogy I think about constantly.

He says executives are like park rangers. 

They're responsible for hundreds of acres of land, and fires are burning everywhere. 

Small brush fires, medium fires, big fires.

They don't have the resources to fight every fire. 

So they focus exclusively on the raging ones and let the smaller ones burn.

Your deal is one of those fires.

If the pain your deal is anchored to is a brush fire, a "nice to solve" problem that's real but not keeping anyone up at night, the executive is going to let it burn. 

Even if they told their VP to "look into it." 

Even if there's budget technically available. 

It will never cross the threshold into a funded initiative because there are bigger fires demanding attention.

This is why so many deals stall after what felt like a promising discovery call. 

The pain was real. 

The buyer was engaged. 

But it wasn't above the noise.

There's a question that tests for this, and you should be asking it midway through every discovery conversation: 

"I want to make sure we're talking about an issue that's high enough on your priority list to actually get attention. When you think about everything on your plate, not just this area but everything, is this something that occupies real mind share? Or is it lower on the list, and we should be talking about something else?"

That question does two things. 

First, it protects you from spending months on a deal anchored to a brush fire. 

Second, it often triggers the buyer to reframe the problem in bigger terms, because they want to justify the time they're spending with you.

Either way, you win. 

You either learn this deal doesn't have enough heat behind it, or your buyer talks themselves into a bigger, more urgent version of the problem.

Selling involves more status dynamics than most reps want to admit.

Humans are constantly calibrating who's who in the social pecking order, and a business conversation is no exception. 

The problem is that most sellers automatically slot themselves below the buyer without realizing they're doing it.

It shows up in subtle ways. 

Ending questions on a high note, almost like you're asking for permission to have asked the question. 

Saying "thank you so much for your time" as if the meeting is a favor the buyer is doing for you. 

Making approval-seeking gestures and filler statements that signal you need the buyer to validate you.

None of this is intentional. 

But your buyer picks up on it immediately, and when they sense that you've placed yourself below them in the pecking order, something shifts. 

They stop seeing you as a peer who can help them solve a business problem and start seeing you as a vendor who needs their business.

The fix is not to be aggressive or domineering. 

It's simpler than that: just don't give your power away.

Maintain a steady, semi-authoritative voice tone. 

When you ask a question, let your inflection go down at the end instead of up. 

Acknowledge that your time is also valuable by framing meetings as mutually beneficial, not as gifts from the buyer.

There's a version of this I see in c-suite meetings specifically. 

A rep walks into a call with a CRO or a CEO and their entire demeanor changes. 

They speed up, they over-explain, they fill every silence.

An executive's time is valuable. 

So is yours. 

Act like it, and watch how differently those conversations go.

Sellers lose thousands of dollars in commission by negotiating price at the wrong time.

The worst version is proactively offering a discount to speed up the deal. 

The second worst is engaging in a pricing conversation while there are still open items in the buying process.

In both cases, you've conceded margin on something that wouldn't have closed the deal anyway.

Here's the habit that fixes it: when your buyer starts pushing on price, respond with this.

"Let's say we agreed on price right now. You're happy with it, I'm happy with it. What would still have to happen before we move forward?"

If they start listing things (legal review, procurement, boss sign-off, security assessment), you stop the negotiation immediately.

"In that case, let's get through those items first. I'd like price to be the very last thing we sort out."

And here's why this matters beyond just protecting your margin. 

When you negotiate price with open items still pending, any one of those items can blow up and restart the entire pricing conversation from a worse position.

But if you handle everything else first and price is genuinely the final gate, the buyer's switching cost to walk away is enormous. 

They've invested weeks of their team's time. 

Legal has reviewed. 

Procurement has been briefed. 

Their boss is expecting the deal.

At that point, you're negotiating from strength.

The only time to engage on price is when the buyer answers your question with "nothing, price is the last thing standing between us and a deal." 

Even then, probe further, because buyers sometimes forget steps in their own buying process. 

A forgotten procurement review that surfaces after you've already conceded on pricing is a very expensive surprise.

One of the habits that quietly kills deals is showing too much product.

We become so excited about what our product can do that we give a full harbor tour of every feature, every workflow, every screen.

The most common discovery question I get isn't about what to ask.

It's "how do I cram all of this into the first call?"

You don't. 

That's the whole answer.

Full discovery uncovers five things: the business pain, its current state, the root cause, the negative impact, and the future state the buyer wants.

Force all five into one 30-minute call and it stops being a conversation. 

It becomes an interrogation, and buyers shut down.

Here's how I actually pace it when I sell.

Call one, 30 minutes. 

I go after two things only.

The business pain and the need behind it. 

Then their perceived root causes, with a little food for thought on causes they haven't considered.

That's a call and a half of the framework, and that's enough.

Call two is the demo, 45 minutes. 

The first seven belong to a summary:

"I want to make sure I'm fiercely aligned with what you're solving. Here's the outcome you're after, and here's what's standing in the way. Did I get that right?"

They confirm, or better, they add context you didn't have.

Then, right before the demo:

"We'll get into the product in a second. But it feels like I missed something last time. What ripple effects is this having on the rest of the business?"

That's negative impact, explored naturally on call two, at the exact moment it feels relevant instead of scripted.

One boundary though. 

Discovery unfolds across the first half of the deal, never the second. 

Ask a brand-new pain question during negotiation and you'll feel the temperature drop.

Front-load what you can. 

Let the rest unfold. 

Complete the picture by call three or four.

Discovery isn't a stage you exit.

It's a picture you finish painting.

How many calls does it take you to see a buyer's full situation?

The two worst questions to ask during sales demos: 1) what questions do you have? 2) does that make sense? 10 questions to ask instead (that actually close deals): 


1. How are you doing [x workflow] today?

Ask this right before you demo a feature that helps with that workflow.

It forces your buyer into a state of contrast:

How they're doing it today vs. how they COULD do it with your product.




2. How does that compare to how you're doing it now?

Same concept as the first question:

Only this time, you ask AFTER you showcased the feature.

Value comes from contrast. This works beautifully.



3. To what extent do you envision this being useful in your situation?

This is a great, non-cheesy way to get them imagining using your product.



4. How do you see your team using that?

Great question to ask decision makers who get VALUE from your product, but don't use it themselves.




5. To what degree is this resonating so far?

Great 'mid point' demo question to ask.

Far better than 'what questions do you have so far?'




6. To what extent do you see that solving [x problem they shared earlier]?

This puts on a 'nail in the coffin' on a pain point they shared.

Demo a piece of your product that solves it.

Then ask that question. It creates mental 'closure' that what you do solves the problem they have.




7. What benefits do you see showing up in your world as a result of what I just showed you?

Easy way to get them voicing the benefits without it sounding like you're trying to hard to make that happen.



8. Where do you want to double-click with a few questions?

Notice this is the same as "What questions do you have?"

Only, the difference in phrasing is a bit of a 'pattern interrupt.'

It makes you seem different than other sellers.

A good thing.



9. It seems like that resonated. What challenge is going on in your business that's making that resonate?

When you sense you 'struck a chord' during your demo, stop and ask that question.

You'll get a treasure-trove of pain and information.



10. It seems like what I just showed you doesn't resonate. Where am I missing the mark?

Ask this when you feel you're OFF the mark.

Get them talking when you sense you're off track.

It's the only way to get BACK on track.

If you're in the first 5 years of your sales career, you probably score a D+ on "business acumen." That's not your fault. Yet. But if you don't fix it:

- you won't get promoted
- people will call you "green"
- you lose credibility with buyers

Here's three things to upgrade your SaaS business acumen:

1. The Rule of 40.

Revenue growth and profitability come at the expense of each other.

The faster you grow, the less profitable you are (usually).

The slower you grow, the more profits you're expected to make.

The rule of 40 measures that trade-off.

Here's how it works:

Take your annual revenue growth rate (say 25%).

Add it to the company's profit margin (say 15%).

Do the numbers add up to 40 (or more)?

Then you're above the rule of 40.

And your growth vs. profitability trade off is healthy.

But let's say your growth rate is 20%.

And your profit margin is 15%.

That business is at 35% compared to the rule of 40.

They have some fixing to do.

2. Net Dollar Retention (NDR).

Let's say your company acquires $100,000 in new ARR in a month.

Now fast forward 12 months.

What has that $100,000 grown (or shrunk) to?

How much of it churned?

How much of it expanded?

Let's say you had $20,000 of it churn.

But then other customers in that group expanded by $50,000.

That means you grew that $100,000 to $130,000 (net).

That makes your net dollar retention 130%.

Which is very healthy.

A baseline healthy NDR is 110%-120%.

If you see 140% and beyond?

That's world class.

This metric matters intensely in SaaS.

Because if you're below 100%?

Then your customer base is shrinking, not growing.

And there will always be more pressure to acquire new customers to plug the leaking boat.

Remember this one.

3. LTV to CAC ratio.

CAC = Customer acquisition costs. 

How much do you spend on sales and marketing to acquire a new customer?

Simple enough.

LTV = Lifetime value. 

How much is an average customer worth during their lifetime to your business.

Ex: $40,000 average annual value, and customers stay for 2.5 years? Your LTV is $100,000.

Now the magic:

Compare your LTV to your CAC.

In a healthy growth business, LTV should be around 3x greater than CAC.
If it's less than that?

A couple things are happening:

- you're spending too much to get customers
- your customers aren't spending enough, long enough

Which makes the economics of your business "suboptimal."

This is the most popular efficiency metric in SaaS.

For good reason.

It's a leading indicator of being able to turn revenue into profit in the future.

There's more.
