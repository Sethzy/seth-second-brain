# Ivory Tang — Forward-deployed engineering as product discovery

## Verbatim Text

Palantir didn’t invent the Forward Deployed Engineer model as a new name for consulting. They invented it because their customers couldn’t tell them what to build.

When your users are intelligence analysts operating inside highly complex organizations, asking them for product requirements doesn’t work. The only way to discover the product is to embed alongside them.

Bob McGrew describes the model simply:

The FDE builds a gravel road for one customer. The core product team turns it into a highway for the next ten.

That distinction is what separates an FDE organization from a services business. A services team asks: How do we deliver this customer’s request efficiently?

An FDE team asks: What can we learn from this deployment that should become part of the platform?

The first implementation might have negative margins. That’s okay if the product absorbs what was learned, the next deployment gets easier, and the company earns the right to take on a larger problem.

This also changes what a “good” customer relationship looks like. In SaaS, we’re trained to standardize contracts, minimize support costs, and sell more seats. In an FDE model, the better health metric may be whether the contract grows over time.

Are you solving more important problems? Are you getting closer to the executive team? Is the customer trusting you with more of the business?

The goal isn’t to make each customer smaller. It’s to make the platform more powerful while expanding the surface area you can own.

There are important guardrails:

1/ Only work on one of the customer’s top executive priorities. Otherwise the deployment won’t survive the organizational resistance required to change how work gets done.

2/ Bring FDEs from multiple accounts into product discussions. A feature should be built from one real workflow, but tested against several others before becoming core product.

3/ Hire product people who can generalize. The hard part isn’t building exactly what one customer wants. It’s finding the abstraction that solves the next 5–10 customers too.

This is why the FDE model is suddenly everywhere in AI. Traditional SaaS usually replaced a known workflow with a better interface. The category already existed. The product discovery had largely been done.

AI agents are different. We have powerful underlying capabilities, but very little agreement on what the actual enterprise products should look like.

The workflows are messy, the organizational barriers are real, and customers often can’t articulate the solution in advance. You have to discover it from inside the enterprise.

That’s why the number of YC companies hiring FDEs has gone from essentially zero to more than 100 in a few years.

McGrew calls the model “doing things that don’t scale, at scale.”

I think that’s exactly right.

The best AI companies won’t avoid services-like work in the beginning. They’ll build an organization capable of converting that work into product—again and again.
