Nine months ago, we introduced two new customer-verifiable exit criteria into the early stages of our sales process.

➡️ S1: Problem Statement: A description of the customer’s current-state problem and the negative consequences it creates.

➡️ S2: 1-Page Business Case A concise business case that restates the problem, outlines the recommended solution, identifies the priority business outcomes and metrics it will support, and clarifies the required investment.

Both have to be co-created with and validated by the customer.

A few benefits we’ve seen:

Tighter Qualification

These exit criteria act as light Champion tests. Can the person we’re working with clearly articulate the business impact of the problem? Are they invested enough to help build the case for change? If not, that is useful information to identify early. Faster disqualification = less time spent on opportunities with a low probability of closing = increased AE productivity.

Higher Average Deal Size

Anchoring opportunities in business pain, negative consequences, and delivery of measurable outcomes, while partnering with a tested Champion early in the process, helps us build larger deals.

Stronger Multi-Product Attach

A problem-first vs. product-first orientation is also helping as Miro continues to add new AI capabilities. When the conversation starts with the customer’s problem rather than a single product, it becomes much easier to configure a platform based solution that delivers the desired outcome. In Q4, 70%+ of our revenue came from new products introduced in the past year, and that trend is continuing into this year.

Credit to Nate Nasralla, whose thinking on 1-Page Business Cases helped inspire the shift in approach.

"My EA sets external calendar invites for 20 minutes, but she actually holds 45 minutes free on my calendar. So, if whoever I'm meeting brings me something interesting, I give them the full 45 minutes.

They've done lots of acquisitions over the years, so this was in the context of vendors, partners, investment bankers, anyone "pitching."

And it's a great question to think about in prep for an exec meeting:

→ Is your point of view* so good, you'll make them late to their next meeting?

_____

*A point of view in "soundbite" form has 4 main parts, in my opinion:

1. Because of [ a major shift / change in their business]
2. Now’s the time to [ respond with this approach ]
3. When you do, [ you'll unlock good outcomes ]
4. While avoiding, [ the cost of bad outcomes ]

Which brings together: 

1. Something that already has built-in momentum / exec mindshare. So you're not trying to manufacture something new for them to focus on, you're "hyperlinking" your convo with what they're actively working on.

2. But, you're introducing some type of non-obvious or hard-won take on *how* to respond to that. Every exec wants to know, "What are others doing, that I'm not, but should consider?" With a degree of specifics.

3. Contrast in the outcomes: value = good - bad outcomes, so, the wider that gap, and more believeably you can describe it and give examples, the more likely you are to build interest in the convo.

Cases are structured ways of building up a POV to guide executives. 

They work by:

1/ Giving you a small set of facts to start.
2/ If you ask the right questions, you get more data.
3/ Then you match the right framework to the situation.
4/ Next, you develop a concise set of recommendations.
5/ Finally, you lay it all out in a concise narrative.

So you have more empathy for your deal champions...

It’s hard to have a meaningful impact on revenue.

Adding $10M in revenue is just 0.0001% of sales for someone like Apple.

Don’t over-index on this in your messaging.

Large companies have A LOT of meetings.

They’re basically corporates deadlines, a way to force feedback.

If prospects need time for extra internal meetings that’s not always bad. It’s normal.

Large companies never have the same urgency sellers do.

Long-time employees have lots of informal clout.

Discover what’s been tried, show you’re different.

Senior execs sign big deals. But they don’t have free time. Like, zero.

#### Verbatim visible post body

I'll tell you with ~80% accuracy if that complex deal you're working hard on will close, based on these 3 things: 

→ Names, numbers, dates.

(1) Names

What's the internal "codename" for the project you're aligned with? 

Don't have a specific, named initiative you're rolled up into? That deal's probably going to stall. But if you do, next question:

Can you pull up the names of who's backing that project as contacts in your cell? Do their names show up in recent text threads?


(2) Numbers 

From inside the buying team, detailing the problem. 

To turn something vague like "churn" into something more vivid, e.g: 

"Our customer LTV fell below $1,000/shopper this year, down from $1,350, since only 40% of loyalty members make more than 2 purchases/year..."


(3) Dates

No exec sets a priority without a target date/deadline. 

If it's meaningful, trust me, it's time-bound. 

So what's the date coming from inside the buying team? Did we break it out into a series of milestones? How are we pacing toward each?


Trying focusing the first ~60 seconds of your next deal review here. 

Even better, start with a written brief on the names, numbers, dates.

#### Verbatim visible post body

The early 2000's sales process is still how ~90% of teams manage deals:

*Qualify → Demo → Proposal → Negotiate → Close*

The issue is the steps you take to sell a product, aren't the same steps your prospects take to buy it. 

Which means 90% of companies have built their entire GTM around their sellers' activity — not internal buying activities. That’s expensive.

Because it means most reps build little to no influence where buying decisions are actually made.

A big part of the answer?

Defining and re-aligning exit criteria at each stage of your sales process, with specific buying behaviors you can “test” for.
