A founder posting twice a year is not founder-led marketing.

The cadence floor is two LinkedIn posts a week, plus quarterly long-form content like newsletters or guest podcasts.

Posts do not distribute themselves. The team has to engage in the first hour.

One founder posting is the start. A team of personal brands is the compounding layer.

## Where teams get founder-led marketing wrong

- Asking the founder to write everything: The founder is the voice, not the production team. They should record loose voice notes or write rough drafts, but the structuring, editing, and scheduling has to live with someone else on the team.

The scoring model you start with is a guess.

You track which scored accounts actually close, then feed the closed-won data back in to refine the weights.

The model gets sharper every quarter instead of staying frozen at whatever you assumed on day one.

## The 3 signal categories

The signals feeding all of this fall into 3 categories. You build the architecture to handle all three.

First-party signals.

The data you own.

Website visits, form fills, email opens and replies, product usage, CRM activity.

Start here before you pay for anything external.

Second-party signals.

Trusted external data from your network.

Partner data, review-site activity, LinkedIn engagement on your team's posts captured through Jungler or Teamfluence.

Not fully yours, but closer to intent than anything public.

Third-party signals.

Public external data.

Technographic changes, job openings, funding announcements, hiring growth.

Noisier than first-party, but they cover a much wider set of accounts and surface companies before they ever touch your site.

You ingest all three into the same source of truth and score them on the same model.

## The 5-step build

Here's how the architecture comes together in order.

Step 1: ingest.

Wire every tool into one source of truth.

Standardize the metadata on every incoming signal so a website visit and a form fill carry the same fields: source, timestamp, account, category.

Step 2: normalize and score.

Assign a point value to every action based on intent level.

Blog visit +2, pricing-page visit +8, demo-form fill +15, product-usage spike +20.

Step 4: trigger.

Build the if/then logic against score thresholds.

High score plus high fit fires a slack alert and an outbound sequence.

Warm but not ready goes to nurture.

Gone quiet goes to retargeting.

Step 5: close the loop.

Tie outcomes back to signals.

Track which scored accounts close, refine the weights from closed-won data, repeat every quarter.

Capture, score, act, measure, adjust.

## How AI changed our 2026 GTM tool stack

A few patterns shifted in the 2026 stack compared to 2024.

The Claygent layer used to be its own category. It moved into Claude Code skills, which now do a lot of what Claygent prompts handled inside Clay.

Standalone copywriting tools used to sit in our content stack. They moved into the LinkedIn post writer skill and the outbound copywriter skill in our Company OS Starter Kit on GitHub.

Manual research tools shrank because Claude Code can now run most one-off research through web scraping MCPs like Firecrawl and Serper.

What grew was the agent infrastructure layer. n8n and Trigger.dev became central as we moved more workflows into terminal-driven execution, with Cursor as the host for most sessions.

For the full thesis on this transition, see the service-as-a-software playbook.

## Where we cut tools that did not make the stack

A list this long looks comprehensive. The cuts matter as much as the inclusions.

We cut:

- Tools that overlapped with what Clay or Apollo already cover

- Tools with no MCP support and no API, because we cannot orchestrate them through Claude Code

- Tools that promised AI but produced output indistinguishable from a generic GPT-4 response

- Tools we used heavily in 2024 but stopped opening in 2025 once Claude Code skills replaced the workflow

The cuts free up budget and team attention for the tools that actually compound. A 30-tool stack used well outperforms a 60-tool stack used at 30 percent.
