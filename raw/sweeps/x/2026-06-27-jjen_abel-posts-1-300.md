18 Lessons in Enterprise Sales ... For Now.

Just a few top-of-mind one-liners ...

1. In a startup's early days, the Founder is the 'product'. Early-adopters buy access to the Founder's vision and trust.

2. Selling the problem is the fastest way to commoditize you. You first need to understand the problem BUT you sell the alpha.  What advantage do they have now because of you.

3. It's all about expansion. Land at $100K-$250K and expand as quickly as possible. This is where you ~10X revenue. The segment itself is quite small.

4. Tier-1 logos are early-adopters. They are encouraged to find alpha opportunities.

5. Outbound sales has the highest contract values. You get to select who you reach-out AND why.

6. The more technical your sales pitch, the faster you'll be delegated down the chain of command. Don't sell user value to an executive.

7. A high win-rate >50% is often a negative signal. It often means you are leaving a TON of money on table OR attacking a very, very small market.

8. Enterprise sales is 1% selling, 99% project managing. You are hand-holding them through buying process - procurement is designed to be arduous to weed out non-serious corporate buyers.

9. SMB is NOT easier than enterprise. Radically different games. SMB is about velocity and marketing-intensive, Enterprise is about high-value, sales intensive.

10. From all of my sales experience, seldom does an intro from anyone (outside a customer) ever close. Ask customers for intros is most reliable & referencable way.

11. Enterprise startups win by turning the exec. into a user. This is often ignored AND easier said than done.

12. Enterprise customers want to know you have a point of view. If you say yes to everything it's erodes trust, big time. No is just as important in conversation.

13. Offering discounts seldom impacts demand, they want more value.

14. Enterprise customers often say 'hardworking tools'. Want to be able to deliver value beyond themselves and to their peers, this is about growing corp. political capital .

15. They get 1-2 at-bats every few years on a new tool. It needs to be a 'no-brainer' for them to buy themselves OR bring to c-suite.

16. Enterprise customers are very, very smart buyers. Do not be cute. Be upfront, be clear, do not incorporate variable pricing.

17. Do not come in saying you save 1M hours OR solve $10MM problems. Make your claims auditable OR it feels really sales-y.

18. Build friendships and get the inside baseball. They should feel the same in-return too. Information advantage wins deals.

below is my shortlist of startup sales learnings over the years ...

these are less obvious and under-discussed

1. 0-1 sales talent does not exist. Founders, this is you.

2. Product/market fit is (almost) always found in adjacent markets. Don’t handcuff yourself to Day 1 market vision.

3. The demo should never be focused on the product — focus on how it increases/grows their influence in the organization.

4. You need to understand their buying process before you build/define your GTM.

5. Unlocking product/market fit is a process of elimination, NOT a hedge. 

6. If the problem is not currently being measured OR managed, it’s likely not chaotic enough to be a priority, today.

7. Specificity is fastest way to build market trust — “Wow, I feel like you understand this better than I do.”

8. The quality of your questions is critical for discovery; the quality of their questions is critical for intent.

9. Your niche is not a random starting point. It’s a GTM strategy to prove the experiment with Act 1.

10. If you don’t own the framing over and over again, their perception will kill your win rate.

11. Give yourself 18-24 months in the market to be invalidated, rejected, and redirected. Founders will also be "Head of Sales" for this period.

12. Enterprise markets wants the ease of PLG-experience, but the project manamengnt and hand-holding of enterprise sales — they want it all and you need to deliver on that.

13. 80% of early sales is getting them excited by YOU, the Founder — how you see the world (vision) and how you uniquely solve their specific problem. Hint: the founder is the 'product.'

14. A seed-stage startup should never have a VP of Sales. Titles matter — fastest way to erode trust with market too.

15. Channel partnerships are a colossal waste of time. If you haven’t sorted it by going direct, don’t expect another party to. 

16. Traction/success abroad seldom puts you further ahead in the U.S.; in fact, it often requires unwinding and relearning.

17. If the primary need is being over-served, focus on the secondary need. This is for ‘red ocean’ markets.

18. The best early-stage sales folks will ‘method-act’ the founder. You need the market to feel the vision in absence of a brand.

19. The sales team should never lead the product roadmap. Share learnings, yes, but never lead.

20. SMB is not easier than enterprise. And, there is no such thing as mid-market — mirage; it's 'upper-end small business' or 'lower-end enterprise'.

21. Never expect the user to sell to the buyer on your behalf — go direct to the buyer -- in many cases, users can be a blocker.

22. If you did not co-write the RFP, you are likely just a checkbox in their buying process.

23. Enterprise deals are all about expansion opportunity -- if you’re not expanding by a multiple YoY, someone is not doing their job

24. In the enterprise, sales get 'closed' via text.

25. One of the fastest routes to shortening enteprise sales cycle is turn the buyer into a user.

26. Most sales talent (> 90%) can’t punch their way out of a paper bag … you’ve been warned ;)

#### Verbatim Text

my shortlist of startup sales learnings over the years ...

1. 0-1 sales talent does not exist. Founders, this is you.

2. Product/market fit is (almost) always found in adjacent markets. Don’t handcuff yourself to Day 1 market vision.

3. The demo should never be focused on the product — focus on how it changes their influence in the organization.

4. You need to understand their buying process before you build/define your GTM.

5. Unlocking product/market fit is a process of elimination, NOT a hedge. 

6. If the problem is not currently being measured or managed, it’s likely not a priority for the prospect, today.

7. Specificity is fastest way to build market trust — “Wow, I feel like you understand this better than I do.”

8. The quality of your questions is critical for discovery; the quality of their questions is critical for intent.

9. Your niche is not a random starting point. It’s a GTM strategy to prove the experiment with Act 1.

10. Building a working GTM will take longer than building a working product.

11. Give yourself 18-24 months in the market to be invalidated, rejected, and redirected. Founders will also be "Head of Sales" for this period.

12. Enterprise markets wants the ease of PLG-experience, but the project manamengnt and hand-holding of enterprise sales — they want it all. 

13. 80% of early sales is getting them excited by YOU, the Founder — how you see the world (vision) and how you uniquely solve their specific problem. Hint: the founder is the 'product.'

14. A seed-stage startup should never have a VP of Sales. Titles matter.

15. Channel partnerships are a colossal waste of time. If you haven’t sorted it by going direct, don’t expect another party to. 

16. Traction/success abroad seldom puts you further ahead in the U.S.; in fact, it often requires unwinding and relearning.

17. If the primary need is being over-served, focus on the secondary need. This is for ‘red ocean’ markets.

18. The best early-stage sales folks will ‘method-act’ the founder. You need the market to feel the vision in absence of a brand.

19. The sales team should never lead the product roadmap. Share learnings, yes, but never lead.

20. SMB is not easier than enterprise. And, there is no such thing as mid-market — mirage; it's 'upper-end small business' or 'lower-end enterprise'.

21. Never expect the user to sell to the buyer on your behalf — go direct to the buyer -- in many cases, users can be a blocker.

22. If you did not co-write the RFP, you are likely just a checkbox in their buying process.

23. Enterprise deals are all about expansion opportunity -- if you’re not expanding by a multiple YoY, someone is not doing their job

24. In the enterprise, sales get 'closed' via text.

25. One of the fastest routes to shortening enteprise sales cycle is turn the buyer into a user.
