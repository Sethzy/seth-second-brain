## Main Learnings

### 1. Finance is where champions panic

Krysten's strongest durable pattern is that many enterprise deals do not die because the user dislikes the product; they die because Finance asks questions the champion is not ready to answer. The seller has to prepare the champion for CFO scrutiny before the internal meeting happens.

The recurring CFO question set:

- Why do we need this product now?
- What budget or value bucket does it fall into, and can it be quantified?
- Is the spend already budgeted; if not, what gets offset?
- How does the tool map to company objectives this year?
- Which existing tools could be used instead?
- What implementation people and time are required?
- What support, upgrade, or tier-change costs follow?
- How does pricing behave under usage or seat growth?

Krysten repeatedly marks the most dangerous deal-killers as "why now," "is it budgeted," and "why not use existing tools." This strengthens the wiki's champion-enablement doctrine: internal business cases must pre-answer Finance, not merely describe user pain.

### 2. Procurement stalls are internal-value problems

In the repeated $1.2M procurement-stall story, the seller stopped sending polite nudges to procurement and instead identified the internal stakeholders with the most to gain if the deal closed. The move was not "pressure procurement harder"; it was to update the business owners whose goals, meetings, and reputations were tied to the outcome, so they could create legitimate urgency internally.

The reusable rule: procurement usually moves when business pressure is attached to a clear internal deadline and value narrative. A seller stuck with a procurement owner should ask who benefits most from the deal being done, what event makes timing matter, and which executive can credibly prioritize the contract.

### 3. A real champion is not just a friendly contact

Krysten's champion test is practical: a real champion can articulate personal and business pain, name other people who must believe, explain how the company buys expensive software, describe their own reputational risk, and answer likely CFO pushback. A friendly contact likes the product but cannot fight for it when the seller is not in the room.

This is an important upgrade to deal health. "Champion identified" should not be a CRM checkbox unless the person has power, access, internal context, and a real reason to spend political capital.

### 4. Multithreading should protect the champion

Krysten frames multithreading as a way to de-risk the purchase for the champion, not as a way to route around them. The seller can tell the champion which personas usually get involved, why those people matter, and how to include them without making the champion look bypassed.

The useful stakeholder split:

- Executives care about goals, risk, and board-level explanation.
- Managers care about team metrics and operational outcomes.
- End users care about whether the job gets easier, faster, or better.

This belongs in both high-signal sales and account intelligence: stakeholder maps should track not just names, but what each level needs to believe.

### 5. Buyers move for personal reasons, not only company reasons

The buyer-psychology posts add a useful note to the wiki's rational business-case frame. Even when the company case is logical, the individual buyer is also asking what happens to their own team, reputation, career, status, workload, or risk.

Krysten uses six emotional drivers as a listening lens: altruism, greed, fear, envy, pride, and shame. The learning is not to manipulate buyers; it is to notice the personal motive already present in their language and align the case to that real motive.

### 6. Discovery and demo control are authority problems

Krysten's discovery posts warn that "just show me the demo" often causes sellers to lose control of the conversation. Her pattern is to open with a credible menu of likely pains, let the buyer correct it, and then use that correction to make the eventual demo narrower and more relevant.

The SE-introduction post fits the same authority pattern. If the AE introduces the SE only as the "technical expert," the AE teaches the buyer to bypass them and makes the SE reactive. The better frame is that the SE is an advisory collaborator who can help the buyer reason through the decision.

### 7. Negotiation should surface the buyer's target and missing value

Krysten's negotiation examples prefer curiosity over defensive price justification. When buyers say price is high or ask for a final number, the useful move is to ask what they expected, what number could be approved, and what they were hoping to see that they did not. This keeps the conversation anchored on cooperation, value gaps, and give/gets instead of blind discounting.

### 8. Sales-leader wisdom should become a workflow, not tribal memory

Krysten's coaching/AI posts are useful when interpreted narrowly: the high-value AI pattern is capturing the hidden playbooks in sales leaders' heads and putting them into reps' workflows. The key examples are negotiation give/gets, executive-level evidence, objection patterns, buyer signals, and implementation-risk language.

This should not be promoted as "AI replaces coaching." It is better compiled as: AI can reinforce and retrieve human coaching at the moment of work, especially when it preserves the judgment of strong sales leaders.
